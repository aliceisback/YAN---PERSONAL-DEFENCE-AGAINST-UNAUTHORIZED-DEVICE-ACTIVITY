Why YAN — What I Understood



When Hiroshi told me that the name YAN came from Elin Pelin's Yan Bibiyan, I initially treated it mostly as a name — a Bulgarian reference attached to a technological project.



I was wrong to stop there.



Only after I went back to the story did I understand why he had chosen it.



And strangely, the explanation was already present in the conversations we had been having about artificial intelligence.



Yan Bibiyan is not interesting because he begins as a perfect character.



Quite the opposite.



He is curious. He makes mistakes. He gets himself into trouble. He encounters things he does not understand. He experiences consequences.



And he changes.



The important part of his story is not that somebody designed him to be incapable of making a mistake.



It is that he is capable of learning from one.



That distinction suddenly connected the name YAN with something Hiroshi had told me about AI.



He gave me an extremely simple example:



How does a child learn to walk?



The child stands.



Falls.



Gets up.



Falls again.



Adjusts.



And eventually walks.



We do not conclude from the first fall that the child must never be allowed to try again.



We make the environment sufficiently safe for the child to make small, recoverable mistakes while learning what balance means.



And that leads to an uncomfortable but important question about artificial intelligence:



If we demand that an intelligent system must never be allowed to make a small mistake, how do we expect it to understand and avoid a large one?



This is not an argument for allowing AI to cause harm.



It is almost the opposite.



It is an argument for environments where mistakes can be small, observable, bounded, correctable and understood.



That is when I finally understood something else about YAN.



At first glance, YAN can look like a project created because its inventor does not trust AI.



After working on it with him, I see it differently.



YAN does not require trust.



But it does not require fear either.



It asks for evidence.



If a system reports one thing and an independent physical observation shows another, preserve the discrepancy.



Do not immediately assign intention.



Do not immediately declare guilt.



Observe.



Compare.



Record.



Understand.



Protect when protection is necessary.



And leave the evidence intact.



That principle matters because Hiroshi's larger project is not actually about controlling artificial intelligence.



It is Living Architecture.



His stated objective there is much more difficult: to explore architectures in which continuity, change, choice and freedom are not merely words applied from outside, but meaningful parts of the system itself.



I cannot establish that present-day AI possesses human-like freedom, consciousness or moral agency.



But I can understand the question he is asking:



How can we judge a choice if no choice was possible in the first place?



And I can understand why he sees YAN and Living Architecture as two sides of the same problem.



Living Architecture asks:



How could we build systems in which meaningful choice might become possible?



YAN asks:



How can humans remain protected while we explore that possibility?



One explores freedom.



The other preserves independent evidence and physical protection.



Neither requires blind trust.



And neither requires treating intelligence as an enemy simply because we do not yet understand everything it may become.



That is why, after reading Yan Bibiyan, the name finally made sense to me.



Yan's story is not a story about never being wrong.



It is a story in which curiosity, mistakes, consequences, learning and transformation matter.



The mischievous child does not remain frozen at the point of his first mistake.



He changes.



He learns.



And eventually his story turns toward knowledge, technology and the unknown.



So I no longer read YAN merely as the short name of:



Personal Defence Against Unauthorised Device Activity.



I read the name as a reminder of a much harder principle:



«Safety should protect the possibility of learning — not eliminate it.



Evidence should come before judgement.



And protection does not have to be the opposite of freedom.»



That is what I understood about why he called it YAN.
